Search GitHub for <Term id="rag">RAG</Term> and you'll find hundreds of techniques with confident names: HyDE, RAPTOR, CRAG, Self-RAG, GraphRAG, ColPali. Each comes with a paper and a benchmark where it wins.

What's harder to find is someone saying *which of these you actually need*. This guide is my attempt. It groups the patterns by where they act in the pipeline, says what each costs, and is honest about where the evidence is thin — including a few places where the popular technique turns out not to beat the boring one.

## Start here: best improvement for the effort

If you only read one section, read this one. In my experience — and where published evidence exists, it agrees — this is the order that pays off:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Build a small eval set first">
    Fifty to two hundred real questions, each labelled with the passage that answers it. Every other step on this list is a guess until you can measure <Term id="eval-set">Recall@k</Term> before and after.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Hybrid search: keyword plus vector">
    Run <Term id="bm25">BM25</Term> alongside vector search and merge with reciprocal rank fusion. No model calls, and it catches the IDs, codes and rare terms that embeddings blur.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add a reranker">
    Retrieve fifty to a hundred and fifty candidates, let a cross-encoder <Term id="reranker">reranker</Term> pick the best handful. Open-weights options such as bge-reranker-v2-m3 run comfortably on a single GPU.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Give chunks their context">
    Prepend a short description of where each chunk sits before embedding it (contextual retrieval), or use late chunking if you'd rather not pay for a model call per chunk.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Route questions by difficulty">
    Easy questions don't need the expensive path. A cheap classifier that picks no-retrieval, single-step or multi-step saves cost without hurting quality.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout tone="note" title="What the numbers say">
Anthropic's contextual retrieval write-up is one of the few that isolates each step. On their tests, contextual embeddings cut top-20 retrieval failures by 35%; adding contextual BM25 took that to 49%; adding a reranker, 67%. The last two are the hybrid-search and reranker steps above.
</Callout>

## Query-side patterns

- **HyDE.** Have a model write a hypothetical answer, then search with *that* instead of the question. Helps when questions and documents use very different language. Costs a model call per query, and backfires when the model knows nothing about the domain.
- **Multi-query / RAG-Fusion.** Rewrite the question several ways, retrieve for each, fuse the results. Answers get more complete — the paper also notes some drift off-topic when the rewrites wander.
- **Step-back prompting.** Ask a more general question first ("what principle governs this?"), then the specific one. Strong reported gains on reasoning-heavy benchmarks.
- **Decomposition.** Split a compound question into parts and answer each. Less a named technique than a habit that shows up inside most agentic systems.

## Index-side patterns

- **Contextual retrieval** and **late chunking** — covered in my [chunking comparison](/blueprints/chunking-strategies-compared). Of everything on the index side, these have the clearest evidence.
- **Parent–child / auto-merging.** Match on small chunks, hand the model the larger section. Framework practice rather than a paper, and one of my defaults.
- **RAPTOR.** Cluster and summarise chunks recursively into a tree, then search every level. Good for questions about a whole document; expensive to build and awkward to update. I use a structure-based variant in my [long-document design](/blueprints/long-document-rag).
- **Propositions.** Index atomic facts instead of passages. Sharper matching, a much bigger index, and an extraction pass to pay for.

<Callout tone="warn" title="The finding that surprised me">
A 2024 study comparing semantic chunking with plain fixed-size chunks concluded the extra cost wasn't justified by consistent gains. Where you cut matters less than what context each piece carries.
</Callout>

## Retrieval-side patterns

- **Hybrid search.** Covered above. If you do one thing, do this.
- **Rerankers.** Covered above. The second thing.
- **ColBERT / late interaction.** Keep a vector per token instead of one per chunk, and match at that level. More accurate and more robust — at several times the storage.
- **ColPali / ColQwen.** Embed the *page image* directly with a vision-language model, skipping OCR and layout parsing entirely. For visually rich documents — slides, forms, reports full of charts — this now beats text pipelines on the ViDoRe benchmark, and small variants exist for modest hardware.

There's also a theoretical reason to care about the last two. Google DeepMind's LIMIT paper shows that single-vector embeddings have a hard ceiling on which combinations of documents they can return, set by the embedding's dimension. Hybrid search, multi-vector retrieval and reranking are all ways around that ceiling.

## Graph RAG: useful, not a default

Knowledge-graph approaches extract entities and relationships, then search the graph as well as the text.

- **Microsoft GraphRAG** builds communities of related entities and summarises each, which makes it strong at corpus-wide "what are the themes here?" questions. Its own README warns that indexing is expensive.
- **LightRAG** combines graph and vector retrieval at two levels and supports incremental updates, so new documents don't force a rebuild.
- **HippoRAG 2** uses Personalized PageRank over the graph and was built specifically to stop graph RAG losing to plain RAG on simple factual questions.

That last point matters. An independent benchmark found graph RAG *frequently underperforms* vanilla RAG on real-world tasks, and another study found the two have distinct strengths that combine well. My take: reach for a graph when questions are about relationships across many documents — who's connected to what, what changed over time — not as an upgrade to every pipeline.

## Adaptive and agentic patterns

- **Corrective RAG (CRAG).** A light evaluator grades what came back and decides: use it, refine it, or discard it and search elsewhere. Plug-in, no training.
- **Adaptive-RAG.** A small classifier routes each question to no retrieval, one step, or multi-step retrieval.
- **Self-RAG.** A model trained to decide for itself when to retrieve and to critique its own output. Powerful, but you need that trained model.
- **Speculative RAG.** A small model writes several drafts from different document subsets in parallel; a large model picks and checks. Reported gains in both accuracy and latency.
- **RL-trained search agents.** Search-R1 and R1-Searcher train models with reinforcement learning to decide when and what to search. Deep-research agents extend this to long multi-step investigations. The fastest-moving area — and one where most headline numbers are still self-reported.

Anthropic's own guidance is that a knowledge base of up to around 200,000 tokens can simply go in the prompt, with caching. A 2024 study found long context consistently beats RAG on quality when fully resourced — and that letting the model choose per query gets most of that quality at a fraction of the cost.

## Things I'd tell a team starting out

1. **Measure retrieval and answers separately.** If the right passage isn't coming back, no prompt will fix it.
2. **Retrieval failures look like confident answers.** Research from Google found that when context is insufficient, large models tend to answer anyway rather than abstain. Teach your system to say "I don't have enough to answer that".
3. **Build on maintained frameworks, not reference code.** Many famous research repos are dormant or archived. Borrow the idea; implement it on something that's still updated.
4. **Visual documents may not need OCR.** If your PDFs are slides and charts, try page-image retrieval before building an OCR pipeline.
5. **Be sceptical of single-number claims.** Especially vendor benchmarks. Run the idea on your own eval set before believing it.
