Every document system eventually meets its first scanned page. Then its first fax, its first phone photo of a form, its first table that spans two columns. <Term id="ocr">OCR</Term> is where most document AI quietly loses accuracy — and because the text looks plausible, nobody notices until retrieval starts missing things.

The good news is that open-source OCR has changed completely in the last two years. Small vision-language models — around a billion parameters — now top the public benchmarks, ahead of models two hundred times their size, and they run on a single GPU. The catch is that no single model is best for every page, and the most accurate ones are hundreds of times slower than the simplest.

So I don't pick one model. I build a pipeline that routes.

## Why route instead of picking the best model

Look at the published numbers and the case makes itself. One widely used toolkit reports its own pipeline at 76% on olmOCR-Bench running about 3 pages a second — and at 44% but 24 pages a second when it skips OCR and trusts the text layer. Same tool, same benchmark: an eight-times speed difference, and a thirty-point accuracy gap.

Neither number is "right". Most pages in a typical corpus are born-digital, where the text layer is perfect and OCR only adds errors. A minority are scans, where the text layer is empty or garbage. Paying for the careful path on every page wastes most of your compute; using the fast path everywhere quietly ruins the scans.

<Callout tone="warn" title="The triage check is not optional">
Text layers lie. PDFs produced by old scanners often carry an invisible, badly OCR'd text layer on top of the image. If you trust every text layer, you inherit someone else's bad OCR. Check the quality before skipping.
</Callout>

A few names worth knowing, grouped by the tier they suit:

- **Classic engines.** Tesseract (CPU only, needs clean 300 DPI input and preprocessing) and PaddleOCR's PP-OCR family, whose newest models range from about 1.5 to 35 million parameters and run fast on CPU.
- **Pipeline toolkits.** Docling (MIT, pluggable OCR engines and a small VLM mode), Marker with Surya (reads the text layer first, OCRs only where needed), MinerU (tiers from model-free to VLM).
- **Small document VLMs.** PaddleOCR-VL and GLM-OCR — both around 0.9B, both layout-first, both near the top of OmniDocBench.
- **Larger VLMs.** olmOCR 2 (7B, Apache-2.0, trained with unit tests as rewards), dots.ocr, and models that specifically claim handwriting and checkbox support.

## Where the real speed comes from

### Serve models properly
Most of the current OCR models ship with support for high-throughput serving runtimes like vLLM or SGLang. Running them page-by-page through a notebook loop wastes most of the GPU. Batch pages, stream them through a server, and keep the GPU busy.

### Compress the model
AllenAI report that olmOCR 2 scores the same in FP8 as at full precision — 82.4 against 82.3. <Term id="quantization">Quantization</Term> to FP8 is close to free for these models. Going further, to 4-bit, is less documented for OCR specifically; measure it on your own pages before trusting it.

### Render at the resolution the model expects
Vision models are trained at particular image sizes. olmOCR expects the longest side at 1288 pixels; Tesseract wants 300 DPI. Rendering bigger doesn't help and costs tokens; rendering at an unfamiliar size quietly hurts accuracy. DeepSeek-OCR makes the trade-off explicit: its paper reports about 97% precision when a page is compressed less than tenfold into vision tokens, falling to around 60% at twentyfold.

### Spend the expensive model where it matters
Escalating only the pages that fail the confidence check is what makes a large model affordable. AllenAI publish a cost of under $200 per million pages for olmOCR 2 on their own hardware — and in a tiered pipeline, only a fraction of pages ever reach that tier.

## A sketch of the router

```python
def ocr_document(pdf):
    out = []
    for page in pdf.pages:
        text = page.text_layer()
        if text and text_quality(text, page) > 0.9:
            out.append(from_text_layer(page))          # tier 0: no OCR
            continue

        img = page.render(longest_side=1288)
        regions = detect_layout(img.downsample())      # blocks, tables, order
        result = [small_vlm.read(img.crop(r)) for r in regions]

        if needs_escalation(result):                   # low confidence,
            result = large_vlm.read_page(img)           # handwriting, bad tables
        out.append(assemble(result, regions, page.number))
    return out

def text_quality(text, page):
    words = tokenize(text)
    dictionary = sum(w.lower() in LEXICON for w in words) / max(len(words), 1)
    coverage = text_area(page) / page.area
    return 0.7 * dictionary + 0.3 * min(coverage * 3, 1.0)
```

## Things that catch people out

- **"Open" doesn't always mean free for business.** Several of the strongest models carry weights licences with revenue thresholds or extra conditions, even when the code is Apache-2.0. Read the model licence, not just the repository licence.
- **The two main benchmarks disagree.** Pipeline tools trail badly on OmniDocBench but stay competitive on olmOCR-Bench. Pick the one that looks like your documents — then build a small benchmark from your own pages anyway.
- **The top of the leaderboard is crowded.** On olmOCR-Bench the leading models sit within a few points of each other. Once you're in that group, speed, hardware fit and licence matter more than rank.
- **A good OCR score isn't a good RAG score.** Recent work found that structural errors — merged columns, broken tables, lost headings — damage retrieval even when character error rates look fine. Evaluate OCR by what it does to your downstream search, not only by edit distance.

## How to evaluate your own pipeline

1. **Sample by page type.** Pull fifty pages each of born-digital, clean scan, degraded scan, tables and handwriting if you have them.
2. **Label a small ground truth.** Correct text for a subset, plus the table structure for table pages.
3. **Measure per tier.** Accuracy and pages-per-second for each route, and what fraction of pages each route receives.
4. **Measure downstream.** Run your retrieval <Term id="eval-set">eval set</Term> on the OCR output. That's the number that matters.
